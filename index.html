Week 1 Technical Foundations ▼
Monday Camera Mastery — Full Manual Control ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can explain how ISO, shutter speed, and aperture interact for video (the "exposure triangle for video")
  • Students understand the 180° shutter rule and can apply it to choose settings
  • Students can shoot in full manual and adjust settings on the fly

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · ISO, Shutter Speed, Aperture for Video
Start with the "180° shutter rule" — shutter speed should be double your frame rate (e.g. 1/50 for 25fps). Explain why this exists: it mimics natural motion blur. Then cover ISO: higher ISO = more noise, especially visible in video. Aperture: shallower depth of field, more background blur. Show real examples — same scene at different settings side by side.
💡 Tip: Use your own camera connected to a monitor/projector so students can see live changes in real time.
0:30 – 1:00 · Frame Rates, Resolution, Picture Profiles
24fps = cinematic feel. 30fps = broadcast/YouTube. 60fps+ = slow motion. 4K vs 1080p — when does resolution matter? Introduce picture profiles (log/flat profiles) — explain why flat footage looks washed out and why grading it gives more dynamic range.
💡 Tip: Show a side-by-side of log footage vs auto-colour footage to make this click instantly.
1:00 – 1:30 · Live Shooting Drill
Set up a simple scene. Each student must manually set ISO, shutter, aperture AND white balance before hitting record. Teacher calls out changes ("the light just changed, adjust!") to simulate real conditions. Debrief: what did they learn about the relationships between settings?

Key Teaching Moment: Students often memorise the settings without understanding WHY. Always ask "what happens to the image if you increase this?" before revealing the answer. The goal is intuition, not formula memorisation.

Discussion Questions to Ask

  • Why can't you just raise ISO if it gets dark? What's the trade-off?
  • You're shooting at 25fps. What shutter speed should you use and why?
  • What does a wide aperture do to your subject vs the background?
  • When would you actually WANT motion blur vs avoid it?

Tuesday Composition & Framing for Video ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can identify and apply the rule of thirds, leading lines, headroom, and look room
  • Students know all major shot types (ECU through WS) and the storytelling purpose of each
  • Students can deconstruct a professional film clip and explain compositional choices

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · Composition Rules
Rule of thirds: the subject should live on the grid intersections, not dead centre (unless intentional). Leading lines guide the viewer's eye. Headroom: too much headroom looks amateurish; too little feels claustrophobic. Look room: when someone is looking to the right, leave space on the right side of the frame. Show BAD examples first — ask students what feels "wrong" about them before revealing the rule.
0:30 – 1:00 · Shot Types
Extreme Close-Up (ECU) = emotion/detail. Close-Up (CU) = face/emotion. Medium Shot (MS) = waist up, conversation. Wide Shot (WS) = location, scale. Extreme Wide (EWS) = establishing. Over-the-Shoulder (OTS) = dialogue. The key lesson: shot type determines emotional proximity. A WS feels cold and distant. A CU feels intimate. Use this deliberately.
1:00 – 1:30 · Film Clip Deconstruction
Play a 2-minute clip from a well-known film or documentary. Pause after each cut and ask students: "What shot type is this? Why did the director choose it here? What does this composition tell us?" Great clips to use: any dialogue scene from a quality drama, or a nature documentary opening.

Key Teaching Moment: Many students know the rules but shoot "centred by default." Have them watch their own previous footage and identify every frame where the subject is dead centre for no reason. This self-critique is highly effective.

  • Why would a director choose an extreme wide shot to start a scene?
  • When is it OK — even powerful — to break the rule of thirds?
  • What's wrong with giving your subject too much headroom?

Thursday Audio Fundamentals ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can identify the right microphone type for different shooting scenarios
  • Students understand proper recording levels and how to monitor audio
  • Students can diagnose and fix common audio problems in post-production

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · Microphone Types
Lavalier (lav): clips on clothing, great for interviews, susceptible to clothing rustle. Shotgun: directional, mounted on camera or boom pole, great for run-and-gun. Condenser (studio): sensitive, picks up everything — use in controlled environments only. KEY RULE: bad audio = unwatchable video. Audiences forgive slightly bad visuals, never bad audio.
0:30 – 1:00 · Recording Levels & Monitoring
Levels should peak at -12dB to -6dB. Never let it hit 0dB (clipping = distortion). Use headphones ALWAYS on set — never trust that it "sounds fine." Room acoustics: hard surfaces = echo (bad). Soft surfaces = sound absorption (good). Always do a room tone recording.
1:00 – 1:30 · Fixing Audio in Post
Walk through common fixes: removing background hum (EQ, noise reduction), de-clicking, normalising levels. Show in DaVinci Resolve or Audacity. Demonstrate: a terrible recording BEFORE and AFTER processing. Lesson: you can rescue bad audio sometimes, but it's always easier to capture clean audio on set.
  • You're filming an interview outdoors on a windy day. What mic do you use and how do you protect it?
  • Your audio levels are peaking at -2dB. Is that a problem? What do you do?
  • You forgot to record room tone. What problems might you face in the edit?

Saturday Lighting for Video ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can set up a three-point lighting rig from scratch
  • Students understand how to "read" and control natural light
  • Students can create different moods using the same subject with different light setups

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · Three-Point Lighting
Key light: main source, positioned 45° above and to the side of the subject. Fill light: softer, opposite side — reduces harsh shadows from key. Backlight (rim light): behind the subject, separates them from the background. Draw this on a whiteboard first. Then demonstrate physically. Show what each light does by turning them on ONE AT A TIME.
0:30 – 1:00 · Natural Light
Golden hour = warm, cinematic. Harsh midday sun = unflattering shadows under eyes and nose. Overcast = free diffused light (professional photographers love this). Window light: side-on = dramatic. Window diffused with a white sheet = soft portrait light. How to use a reflector to bounce natural light as a fill.
1:00 – 1:30 · Practical Setups
Hands-on: students set up and shoot an interview with 3-point lighting. Then try a 1-light setup for a dramatic/moody look. Compare the two. Discuss: which feels more "professional"? Which tells a different story? Neither is wrong — context matters.
  • What does a fill light actually do — and what happens if it's too bright?
  • You're shooting outdoors at noon with harsh shadows. What do you do?
  • How would you light someone to look villainous vs heroic?

Week 2 Production Skills ▼
Monday Interview & Talking Head Setup ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can set up a professional-looking interview from scratch in under 10 minutes
  • Students can direct a non-actor to give natural, usable responses
  • Students understand eye-line and why it matters in documentary/interview filmmaking

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · Setup: Camera Angles, Eye-Line, Background
Camera slightly above eye level = authority. Eye level = equal/honest. Below = power (use for villain/CEO effect). Eye-line: subject should look slightly OFF camera (at the interviewer beside the lens), NOT directly into lens — unless you WANT that direct audience address effect. Background: rule of thirds applies — place something interesting but not distracting behind the subject at a slight distance so it goes out of focus.
0:30 – 1:00 · Directing Non-Actors
Most people freeze when a camera points at them. Strategies: (1) Ask warm-up questions not in the film — get them talking before you roll. (2) Ask them to repeat your question in their answer ("So the project started when..."). (3) If they stumble, say "great — let's do that last part again" (never "you messed up"). (4) Silence is OK — let them finish their thought, don't rush in.
1:00 – 1:30 · Mock Interview Shoot
Pair students up. One is the camera operator/director, one is the subject. 5-minute interview on any topic. Then swap. Teacher watches and gives real-time feedback. Focus on: audio quality, background, eye-line, natural conversation. Debrief together.
  • Why does your subject look uncomfortable on camera and what can you do about it?
  • What does it mean when someone "looks into the lens" — when is that good or bad?
  • How do you choose a background for an interview?

Tuesday B-Roll Strategy & Visual Storytelling ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand what makes effective B-roll (vs random filler shots)
  • Students can plan a B-roll shot list for any given project
  • Students can edit B-roll over interview audio with smooth rhythm

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · What Makes Good B-Roll
B-roll is NOT filler — it's visual evidence that supports what the narrator/subject is saying. Good B-roll rule: if the audio says "we worked through the night," the B-roll should SHOW people working at night. Bad B-roll: random shots of computers, hands typing that have nothing to do with the story. The three cutaway types: inserts (close-up of a detail), reaction shots (person listening), context shots (the wider environment).
0:30 – 1:00 · B-Roll Shot Planning
Show how to write a B-roll shot list: start with the interview transcript, highlight every noun and action, each one is a potential B-roll moment. Example: "we designed the logo in Illustrator" → B-roll: hands on keyboard, Illustrator on screen, close-up of the final logo. Teach the rule of threes: wide, medium, close of every important subject gives you editing flexibility.
1:00 – 1:30 · Live Editing Demo
Teacher edits B-roll over interview audio live on screen. Show how B-roll cuts cover awkward pauses, remove "ums," and dramatically improve pacing. Let students try cutting a short sequence with provided footage if available.
  • What's the difference between B-roll and cutaways?
  • How do you plan B-roll before you shoot — what's the process?
  • When should B-roll NOT be used?

Thursday Camera Movement Techniques ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand when to use tripod, handheld, and gimbal — and why the choice is intentional
  • Students can execute pans, tilts, push-ins, pull-outs, and tracking shots
  • Students can motivate camera movement with story logic — not just for visual variety

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · Stabilisation Methods
Tripod = stability, precision, formality. Handheld = energy, urgency, intimacy (used in documentary, action, horror). Gimbal = smooth movement with slight "floatiness" — great for following subjects. KEY LESSON: every movement should feel motivated. Handheld in a drama feels wrong; handheld in a heist feels right. The tool matches the emotion you're trying to create.
0:30 – 1:00 · Movement Types
Pan: horizontal rotation (following a moving subject). Tilt: vertical rotation (revealing height). Push-in (dolly in): camera moves physically closer — increases tension, focus. Pull-out: camera moves away — reveals context, creates distance. Tracking shot: camera moves alongside subject — creates connection. Demonstrate each. Then: WRONG USES (random pans that serve no story purpose).
1:00 – 1:30 · Movement Exercise
Students shoot the same 20-second scene 3 ways: static/tripod, handheld, and one intentional movement (their choice). Compare all three. Which feels most appropriate? Why? This hands-on comparison is very effective.
  • Why does handheld feel wrong in a wedding video but right in a news report?
  • What is a "motivated" camera move? Give an example of a push-in that's emotionally motivated.

Saturday Multi-Camera Setups ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can sync footage from two or more cameras using clapperboard or audio sync
  • Students understand strategic camera placement for live events and multi-cam shoots
  • Students can edit a multi-cam sequence with clear cutting logic

How to Teach This Session (1h 30min)

0:00 – 0:30 · Syncing Footage
Clapperboard: the sharp visual/audio spike makes syncing easy. Hands clap: same principle, free. Timecode: for professional productions. In editing (Premiere/DaVinci): sync clips by waveform or manually align to the clap. Demonstrate this live. Create a multicam sequence in the NLE so students see how it works.
0:30 – 1:00 · Covering Live Events
Camera A: main/wide establishing angle — always running, the safety net. Camera B: close-ups, reaction shots, detail. Camera C (if available): creative/roaming. The rule: Camera A never stops recording. You can miss a close-up; you cannot miss the whole moment on the wide. Discuss: church event, conference, performance — where do you place each camera?
1:00 – 1:30 · Multi-Cam Edit Workshop
Using footage from the session (pairs shooting from 2 angles), walk through the multi-cam editing process. Key editing principle: cut to the most interesting or emotionally appropriate angle, not just for variety. Let students edit their own sequence.
  • If one of your cameras runs out of battery mid-event, which one should it NOT be and why?
  • When editing multicam, when do you cut — on the action, before it, or after?

Week 3 Post-Production ▼
Monday Editing Workflow — Premiere / DaVinci ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students set up a proper project structure before editing (bins, proxies, naming)
  • Students understand pacing and "the invisible edit" — cuts that feel natural
  • Students can cut a coherent 2-minute piece from raw footage
0:00 – 0:30 · Project Setup & Organisation
Before you touch the timeline: create a folder structure (RAW Footage / Audio / Music / Exports). In the NLE: bins for interview, B-roll, music, SFX. Proxies: for 4K footage that's too heavy to edit smoothly — create lower-res proxy files. Naming: "PROJ_SCENE_SHOT_TAKE" (e.g., DOC01_INT001_WS_T03). This matters enormously on collaborative or long-term projects.
0:30 – 1:00 · Pacing & The Invisible Edit
The best editing is invisible — the viewer doesn't notice the cuts. Principles: cut on action (cut as the subject moves). Cut on emotion (the moment expression changes). Match cut (similar shapes/movements between two shots). J and L cuts (audio starts before the cut, or continues after). Show the same scene cut two ways: one with cuts on random points, one with "invisible" editing principles applied.
1:00 – 1:30 · Editing Drill
Students cut a 2-minute piece from raw footage (their own from Week 2 or provided footage). Teacher circulates, advises. Focus: every shot should earn its place. If a clip doesn't add information, emotion, or pacing — cut it.
  • Why create proxies? When would you NOT need them?
  • What does "cutting on action" mean? Why does it feel more natural to the viewer?

Tuesday Colour Theory for Videographers ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can read and use a waveform, vectorscope, and parade display
  • Students understand the emotional meaning of warm vs cool colour temperatures
  • Students can perform a basic colour correction (not grading) on any clip
0:00 – 0:30 · Colour in Storytelling
Warm tones (orange/amber) = comfort, nostalgia, warmth. Cool tones (blue/teal) = coldness, isolation, professionalism. This is why medical dramas look blue and romantic comedies look warm. Show film stills: The Grand Budapest Hotel (pinks/purples), No Country for Old Men (desaturated orange). Ask: what is the film trying to make you feel? Skin tones are the anchor — correct those first, always.
0:30 – 1:00 · Reading Scopes
Waveform: shows luminance (brightness). Highlights at top, shadows at bottom. Nothing should clip at 0 or 100. Vectorscope: shows colour saturation and hue. The skin tone line is crucial — all human skin tones should fall on this diagonal line regardless of ethnicity. Parade: RGB channels separately — useful for colour balancing. Practical demo: open a clip with obvious colour problems and use scopes to diagnose it.
1:00 – 1:30 · Colour Correction Practice
Students work through correcting a problem clip: fix exposure using waveform, fix white balance by eyeballing skin tones on the vectorscope, ensure no clipping. The goal is "neutral and correct" — not creative. That comes in the grading session.
  • What's the difference between colour correction and colour grading?
  • Why do you fix skin tones first when colour correcting?
  • What does it mean when the waveform clips at the top?

Thursday Colour Grading Basics ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students know the difference between colour correction (technical) and colour grading (creative)
  • Students can create a simple cinematic colour look using shadows, mids, highlights, and saturation
  • Students can match a grade to a reference image
0:00 – 0:30 · Correction vs Grading
Correction = making the footage technically accurate (correct exposure, neutral white balance, proper skin tones). Grading = applying a creative, stylistic look on top of correction. You ALWAYS correct before you grade. Show: corrected footage → then the same footage with an orange-teal grade (the most popular cinematic look) → then the same footage with a cold/desaturated documentary look. Same footage, three completely different films.
0:30 – 1:00 · Creating a Look
The "teal and orange" look: push shadows toward teal/blue, push highlights/mids toward orange/warm. This works because teal and orange are complementary — and skin tones are naturally orange, so they pop against a teal shadow. Walk through the node/layer structure in DaVinci. Show how saturation changes the intensity. Lift the blacks slightly (creative choice to reduce contrast — gives a "film" feel).
1:00 – 1:30 · Match Grade to Reference
Each student picks a reference image (from a film or photographer). They try to match it. No copy-pasting LUTs — the goal is to understand what sliders to move and why. Teacher circulates and asks: "What colour is in the shadows? What's the saturation level? Are the highlights warm or cool?"
  • Why is it important to correct before you grade?
  • What are the three tonal regions in any image and which controls affect each?

Saturday Sound Design & Music Selection ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand the role of ambient sound, SFX, and music in storytelling
  • Students can choose music that serves the story rather than just sounds good
  • Students can mix dialogue, music, and SFX at appropriate relative levels
0:00 – 0:30 · Sound Design Layers
Every professional video has three sound layers: Dialogue/narration (most important — always the loudest). SFX (sound effects — footsteps, door clicks, ambient sounds that match the action). Music (emotional underpinning — should be felt, not heard fighting for attention). Demo: play a clip with no audio → add ambient sound → add SFX → add music. Each layer adds emotional depth.
0:30 – 1:00 · Choosing the Right Music
The test: does the music serve the story, or is it just a track you like? Music that "tells" the audience how to feel before the image does = lazy. Music that enhances and amplifies what's already in the image = great. Resources: Epidemic Sound, Artlist, YouTube Audio Library (free). Tempo: fast edit → fast music. Emotional scene → space in the music. Key: music and edit rhythm should feel like they're talking to each other.
1:00 – 1:30 · Audio Mixing
Levels: dialogue at -6 to -3dB. Music at -18 to -12dB (under dialogue). SFX between the two depending on purpose. Ducking: music automatically lowers when dialogue starts. Key rule: close your eyes. If you can still follow the story just by listening, your mix is working. If dialogue gets lost under music, it's broken.
  • What does "ducking" mean in audio mixing?
  • You have a powerful emotional scene. Should you use a dramatic orchestral track or silence with ambient sound? Why might either choice be right?

Capstone Checklist — Before You Submit

  • Video is 1.5–3 minutes in length
  • Proper exposure — no clipping highlights or crushed blacks
  • Audio is mixed correctly — dialogue clear, music not overpowering
  • Colour grade is consistent across all shots
  • Composition is intentional — no accidental centred subjects
  • Story has a clear beginning, middle, and end (even if abstract)
  • Shot list is submitted alongside the film
  • Exported at 1080p minimum, H.264, correct aspect ratio
✍️ Self-Review Before Submission

Watch your film with the sound OFF. Does it still tell a story visually? Then watch it with your eyes CLOSED. Does the audio alone carry emotion and information? If both answers are yes, you have a well-crafted piece. If either fails, revisit that element before submission.

Week 1 Technical Mastery ▼
Monday Exposure Triangle — Deep Dive ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand how ISO, shutter speed, and aperture interact and affect each other
  • Students can use metering modes and read a histogram to get correct exposure
  • Students can make deliberate creative exposure choices (expose for highlights vs shadows)
0:00 – 0:30 · The Triangle
ISO: sensor sensitivity. Raises brightness but adds noise. Key trade-off. Shutter speed: controls motion. Fast (1/1000) = freeze action. Slow (1/15) = motion blur. Aperture: f-stop. Low f-number (f/1.8) = wide open, shallow depth of field (blurry background), more light. High f-number (f/16) = deep focus, everything sharp, less light. Key insight: each setting has a creative SIDE EFFECT beyond just brightness. Teach the side effect, not just the exposure relationship.
0:30 – 1:00 · Metering & Histograms
Evaluative/matrix metering: reads the whole scene. Spot metering: reads only a small circle — useful for high-contrast scenes. The histogram: left = shadows, right = highlights, middle = midtones. A spike at the far right edge = clipped highlights (detail lost). A spike at far left = crushed blacks. "Expose to the right" technique: intentionally expose slightly bright (without clipping) to preserve shadow detail in RAW files.
1:00 – 1:30 · Shooting Drill
Students photograph a high-contrast scene (window with bright light outside). Task 1: expose for the bright outside (interior goes dark). Task 2: expose for the interior (outside clips). Task 3: find the balanced exposure. Discuss: there is no "correct" — only intentional choices.
  • You're shooting a sprinting athlete indoors in low light. Which two settings are in conflict and why?
  • What does a histogram with everything piled on the left side tell you?
  • When would you DELIBERATELY overexpose for creative effect?

Tuesday RAW Shooting & File Management ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand the real-world difference between RAW and JPEG and why it matters
  • Students have a consistent file naming and folder structure system
  • Students can cull a large shoot down to selects efficiently using a rating system
0:00 – 0:30 · RAW vs JPEG
JPEG: camera bakes in sharpening, noise reduction, colour, and compression. Small file. Limited editing. RAW: all sensor data preserved. Much larger file. Requires editing software (Lightroom, Capture One). Shows its value when: recovering overexposed highlights, adjusting white balance dramatically, pulling detail from dark shadows. Demo this live: show a RAW and JPEG of the same slightly over-exposed shot edited side-by-side. The RAW recovery will be dramatic.
0:30 – 1:00 · File Organisation
Folder structure: Year → Month → Project name (e.g. 2025 → 05-May → Brand_Shoot_ClientX). File naming: YYYYMMDD_ProjectName_0001.RAF (or .CR3 etc). Two backups minimum: local hard drive + cloud. The 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, on 2 different media types, 1 offsite. Never edit original files — always work from copies or catalogued edits in Lightroom (non-destructive).
1:00 – 1:30 · Culling Workflow
Culling = selecting the best images from a shoot quickly. Step 1: quick pass — flag obvious rejections. Step 2: star rating 1–5 for remaining. Step 3: only work on 4-star and 5-star images. In Lightroom: use the keyboard (P = pick, X = reject, 1–5 for stars). Pro speed: never cull on camera screen — too slow and battery-draining. Import first, cull on monitor.
  • If a client urgently needs images in 1 hour, do you shoot RAW or JPEG? Why?
  • What is non-destructive editing and why does it matter?

Thursday Lenses — Choosing the Right Glass ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can explain focal length compression and perspective distortion
  • Students understand when to choose a prime vs zoom lens
  • Students have shot the same subject with two different focal lengths and can articulate the difference
0:00 – 0:30 · Focal Length & Perspective
Wide angle (16–35mm): dramatic, distortion at edges, makes spaces look bigger, great for architecture and landscapes. Normal (50mm): closest to human eye — natural perspective. Short telephoto (85mm): ideal for portraits — flattering compression, no distortion. Long telephoto (200mm+): compresses background dramatically, isolates subject, great for sports/wildlife. KEY DEMO: photograph the same face from the same position at 24mm vs 85mm. The distortion at 24mm makes portraits unflattering.
0:30 – 1:00 · Prime vs Zoom
Prime (fixed focal length): forces you to move physically — develops compositional thinking. Usually sharper and faster (wider max aperture) than zooms at the same price point. Zoom: convenience, versatility, great for events where you can't move. False belief: "zoom = lazy." Reality: many professional photographers use zooms. The right tool for the context.
1:00 – 1:30 · Focal Length Comparison Shoot
Students photograph one subject (a person or object) using two different focal lengths. Compare side by side. Ask: which feels more natural? Which creates more drama? Which is more flattering for a portrait? The visual difference is always more persuasive than any explanation.

Saturday Composition — Rules & When to Break Them ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students know the core composition rules and can apply them deliberately
  • Students can identify when breaking a rule creates a stronger image
  • Students have developed a personal way of thinking about composition
0:00 – 0:30 · Core Rules
Rule of thirds: place subject on grid intersections. Symmetry: powerful for architecture, reflections — but only works if the symmetry is PERFECT. Leading lines: roads, rivers, walls that draw the eye to the subject. Negative space: deliberate emptiness that gives the subject room to breathe and creates mood. Frame within a frame: use doorways, windows, trees to create a secondary frame around your subject.
0:30 – 1:00 · Studying Great Photographers
Show 10 powerful images from photographers like Steve McCurry, Sebastião Salgado, Annie Leibovitz, or street photographers like Vivian Maier. For each: what composition rule did they use? Did they break one? Why does it work despite (or because of) breaking the rule? Rule-breaking works when: there is a deliberate reason. It fails when: it's accidental.
1:00 – 1:30 · Composition Challenge
Students shoot 5 images each using a different rule for each. Then attempt to break one rule intentionally — and make it work. The key: when you break a rule, you must be able to explain WHY that break is stronger than following it.
  • Placing the subject dead centre is technically "wrong" — but name two situations where it would be the most powerful choice.
  • What makes negative space work? When does it just look like poor composition?

Week 2 Lighting & Shooting Styles ▼
Monday Natural Light Photography ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can identify and use golden hour, blue hour, and overcast light for different effects
  • Students can work with window light as a directional portrait light source
  • Students have shot a natural light portrait session with intentional light decisions
0:00 – 0:30 · Light Throughout the Day
Golden hour (sunrise/sunset): warm, directional, long shadows — most flattering for portraits and landscapes. Harsh midday: direct overhead sun = deep eye shadows, unflattering for faces. But great for: high contrast street photography, food, architecture. Blue hour (just before sunrise/after sunset): cool, soft ambient light, beautiful for architecture. Overcast: natural diffusion = even, shadowless light — beloved by portrait and product photographers. The key: understand what each light does, then choose when to shoot based on what the image needs.
0:30 – 1:00 · Window Light
Side-on window = directional, dramatic, beautiful shadows (Rembrandt lighting can be achieved naturally). Reflector or white card on the shadow side fills shadows softly. Net curtain or white sheet over window = diffused, even, studio-quality light for free. Position subject at 45° to window for the most versatile portrait light. Demonstrate each variation with a volunteer subject.
1:00 – 1:30 · Outdoor Portrait Session
Students photograph a subject outdoors. Challenge: find three different natural light scenarios within the same location (shade = soft, open sky = diffused, direct sun = edgy). For each: what does the light do to the face? What mood does it create? When would each be the right choice?

Tuesday Artificial Lighting — Flash & Strobes ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand the difference between on-camera and off-camera flash and when to use each
  • Students can identify and use light modifiers (softbox, umbrella, bounce card) appropriately
  • Students have set up and photographed with a 1-light and 2-light portrait setup
0:00 – 0:30 · On-Camera vs Off-Camera Flash
On-camera flash (direct): flat, harsh, causes red-eye — avoid for portraits. On-camera flash (bounced off ceiling/wall): immediately softer and more natural. Off-camera flash: any direction, any modifier — total creative control. Sync speed: most cameras max sync at 1/200–1/250. Above that = dark banding on the image. Crucial to teach this — it's a common beginner mistake.
0:30 – 1:00 · Modifiers
Larger modifier = softer light. Key modifiers: Softbox (rectangular, diffused, directional — studio standard). Umbrella (reflective or shoot-through — cheap, easy, slightly less control). Beauty dish (sharp but flattering — great for fashion/beauty). Reflector/bounce card: redirects existing light as a free fill. Demonstrate: same flash, different modifier = completely different quality of light on the subject.
1:00 – 1:30 · Studio Portrait Setup
Students set up a 1-light (key only) and 2-light (key + fill/hair light) portrait. Shoot 10 frames each. Compare: how does adding the second light change the mood? What does a hair/rim light do? When would you want more shadow vs less?

Thursday Portrait Photography ▼

Session Objectives

0:00 – 0:30 · Posing & Direction
Most people freeze when photographed — your job is to make them feel comfortable and give them direction. Never say "stand there and smile." Instead: give them an action ("pick up that cup"), a thought ("imagine you just got some great news"), or a position ("chin slightly down, look here"). Environmental portrait: subject in their natural space (office, studio, home, street) — tells the story of who they are. Studio portrait: controlled, clean, all attention on the person — great for headshots and editorial. Neither is better — they answer different questions. Choose based on: what story are you trying to tell about this person?

Pairs: 10-minute portrait session each. Teacher circulates. Key checklist: Is the light flattering? Is the subject comfortable? Are you directing them or just clicking? Review together: what made the good frames good? What made the weak ones weak?

Monday Lightroom Workflow ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can import, organise, and catalogue photos properly in Lightroom
  • Students can perform all basic adjustments to achieve a clean, natural edit

0:00 – 0:30 · Import & Organisation
Import settings: always copy (never move) RAW files to organised folders. Apply develop settings on import (basic lens correction + remove chromatic aberration). Collections: like playlists for your photos — non-destructive grouping. Smart collections: dynamic groups based on criteria (5-star rating, red label, etc.). Teach students to add keywords on import — saves hours searching later.
0:30 – 1:00 · Basic Adjustments
The order of adjustments matters: (1) White balance (temperature + tint). (2) Exposure. (3) Highlights + Shadows. (4) Whites + Blacks. (5) Contrast/Clarity. (6) HSL (hue, saturation, luminance of specific colours). (7) Sharpening + Noise reduction. Show: the same unedited photo, adjusted poorly (over-edited, plastic-looking) vs adjusted well (natural but polished). The goal is "it looks like this photo was taken well" not "I edited this aggressively."
1:00 – 1:30 · Consistent Editing Across a Set
Select the "hero" image of a set and edit it perfectly. Then: Sync settings to all photos in the set. Fine-tune each (exposure may vary slightly shot to shot). The goal: a viewer should not be able to tell which was edited first or last. All photos should feel like they were taken at the same moment with the same intention.
  • Why do you adjust white balance BEFORE exposure — not after?
  • What's the difference between Clarity and Texture in Lightroom?

Tuesday Retouching in Photoshop ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can use healing brush, clone stamp, and spot removal naturally
  • Students understand frequency separation for skin retouching that looks real
  • Students can composite: select a subject and replace the background
0:00 – 0:30 · Healing & Cloning
Spot healing brush: click a blemish — Photoshop automatically blends the surrounding texture. Healing brush: you define the source sample, then apply. Clone stamp: exact pixel copy — useful for very specific repairs. KEY lesson: always work on a NEW LAYER when retouching. Never touch the original. Show: over-retouched portrait (waxy, plastic skin) vs well-retouched portrait. The goal is "they look like themselves on a great day" not "they look like a rendering."
0:30 – 1:00 · Frequency Separation
Separates texture (pores, hair detail) from tone/colour. You can smooth uneven skin tones on the low-frequency layer without affecting skin texture. Demonstrate the setup: duplicate layer twice, apply Gaussian blur to the bottom copy, apply Apply Image to the top. Work on the low-frequency layer with a soft healing brush for natural skin. This technique is the industry standard for beauty retouching.
1:00 – 1:30 · Background Replacement
Select Subject (Photoshop AI): remarkably fast, good for most subjects. Refine Edge/Select and Mask: for hair and complex edges. Remove Background button (newer Photoshop). Best practice: shoot subjects against a plain, contrasting background when you know you'll composite later. Demonstrate: place subject on new background. Key: match the light direction and quality. A subject lit from the left can't be placed on a background lit from the right.

Capstone Portfolio Checklist

  • Exactly 10 images — no more, no less (curation is part of the assessment)
  • Images feel cohesive in colour, mood, and style
  • Each image has a title and 1–2 sentence description/technical note
  • No accidental blur, missed focus, or incorrect exposure in any selected image
  • Images exported at print-ready resolution (300 DPI) AND web resolution
  • At least 3 different genres represented (e.g. portrait, product, event)
  • Presented as a PDF or Lightroom gallery

Start with 50 of your best images from the 4 weeks. Cut to 20 (your absolute best). From 20, cut to 10 — but ask: do these 10 images tell a story about who I am as a photographer? Do they show range? Do they show consistency? The 10 that make the most coherent set is better than the 10 that are individually best.

Monday Core Design Principles ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can name and apply the 5 core design principles: hierarchy, contrast, balance, alignment, repetition
  • Students can analyse a real design and identify which principles are present or missing
  • Students can redesign a poorly-made piece using the principles as a checklist
0:00 – 0:30 · The 5 Principles
Hierarchy: the most important element should look the most important (size, weight, colour). The viewer's eye should move through the design in the intended order. Contrast: difference in size, weight, colour, shape — creates visual interest and draws attention. Balance: symmetrical (formal, stable) or asymmetrical (dynamic, modern). Alignment: every element should align to something — invisible grid lines. Never "near the edge" — either properly aligned or intentionally offset. Repetition: repeating visual elements (colours, fonts, shapes) creates unity and consistency.
0:30 – 1:00 · Good vs Bad Design Analysis
Show 5–10 real designs: some terrible (cluttered church flyers, over-designed posters), some excellent (Apple product pages, elegant event invitations). Design critique is a skill — teach the language.
1:00 – 1:30 · Redesign Exercise
Each student gets the same terrible design. They redesign it in Figma or Canva in 25 minutes. Then they share with the group and explain every change they made and which principle it applies. There is no single "correct" redesign — evaluate how well they can articulate the reasoning.
  • What's the difference between hierarchy and emphasis? Are they the same thing?
  • You have a poster where everything is the same size and colour. Which principle is most broken?
  • Can a design have too much contrast? What does that look like?

Tuesday Typography Mastery ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can identify type classifications and explain when each is appropriate
  • Students understand font pairing logic and can choose two fonts that work together

0:00 – 0:30 · Type Classifications & Pairing
Serif (e.g. Times, Playfair): traditional, authoritative, elegant. Sans-serif (e.g. Helvetica, Inter): modern, clean, friendly. Display/Script: expressive, decorative — use sparingly, for headlines only. Monospace: code, technology aesthetic. Font pairing rules: pair a serif headline with a sans-serif body (or vice versa). Never pair two fonts that are too similar (creates visual confusion). Use contrast — different enough to feel intentional, related enough to feel cohesive. Google Fonts Pairing tool is a free resource.
0:30 – 1:00 · Micro-Typography
Kerning: space between individual letter pairs. Bad kerning looks amateurish. Leading: line spacing. Too tight = hard to read. Too loose = feels disconnected. Tracking: letter-spacing across whole words. All-caps with increased tracking = professional. All-caps with no tracking = amateur. Line length: 50–75 characters per line is optimal for reading comfort. Hierarchy with type: size contrast, weight contrast (bold vs light), colour contrast — at least 2 of these should be used.
1:00 – 1:30 · Type-Only Poster Challenge
No images, no shapes, just type. Design a poster for a quote, event, or announcement. All visual interest must come from font choice, size contrast, weight, colour, and placement. This is harder than it sounds — it forces students to understand typography deeply. Share results: discuss what works and why.
  • What's the practical rule about how many fonts you should use in one design?
  • Why does small all-caps text with tight tracking look so different from all-caps with loose tracking?

0:00 – 0:30 · Slide Design Principles
The worst presentations: walls of text, too many bullet points, low-contrast text on busy backgrounds, 3D charts, WordArt. The best presentations: one clear idea per slide, bold typography, meaningful visuals, high contrast. Rule: if you can read everything on the slide during the presentation, the presenter is redundant. The slide should enhance what you're saying, not replace you. Show: same content in a bad deck vs a well-designed deck. The difference is always dramatic.
0:30 – 1:00 · Data Visualisation

Monday Intro to Motion Graphics ▼

Session Objectives

0:00 – 0:30 · What Are Motion Graphics
Motion graphics = graphic design + movement + time. Used in: title sequences, explainer videos, social media ads, lower thirds in broadcasts, logo animations, UI transitions. Show examples: a simple lower third (name + title banner), a social media ad with animated text, a kinetic typography piece (lyrics or quotes animated). Key distinction: motion graphics tell stories through designed animated elements — different from live action filmmaking.
0:30 – 1:00 · Animation Basics
Keyframes: a keyframe marks a specific state of an element at a specific time. Between keyframes = the animation. In Canva: Animate button, choose animation style per element. In After Effects: select layer, press P (position), S (scale), R (rotation), T (opacity) to reveal keyframeable properties. Set keyframe 1 → move to a later point in time → change the value → After Effects interpolates between them. Start with simple: fade in, scale up, slide in. Keep animations purposeful — movement should mean something.
1:00 – 1:30 · Logo Animation
Students animate their logo from Week 2. 3–5 second reveal. Must feel intentional — the animation should reflect the brand's personality. Tech brand: sharp, fast, geometric movement. Spa brand: slow, elegant, organic movement. Export as GIF and MP4. Critique: play all animations and discuss — does the movement match the brand?

Tuesday Animation Principles for Designers ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand easing, timing, anticipation, and follow-through as animation principles
  • Students can create animated text and lower thirds
  • Students have built a 15-second animated social media ad
0:00 – 0:30 · The Disney Principles (for Motion Designers)
Easing: nothing in the real world moves at a constant speed (linear). Objects accelerate and decelerate — this is "ease in" and "ease out." Ease in = starts slow, ends fast (like throwing a ball). Ease out = starts fast, ends slow (like landing). In After Effects: Graph Editor lets you control the velocity curve. Anticipation: a small reverse movement before the main action — adds weight and believability. Squash and stretch (subtle): makes objects feel like they have physical mass. Follow-through: elements overshoot slightly then settle — rather than stopping dead.
0:30 – 1:00 · Text Animations & Lower Thirds
Text animation: per-character animation in After Effects (Animator tool). Common styles: type on, slide up, fade + scale. Lower thirds: the name/title banners used in news and documentaries. Design a lower third: background shape animates in, then text types on (or slides in) over it. Duration: 2–3 seconds in, hold for 3–5 seconds, then out. Keep it clean — lower thirds should never fight with the video behind them.
1:00 – 1:30 · 15-Second Social Ad
Students build a complete 15-second animated social media ad for any product. Must include: animated text, at least one image or graphic element, transitions between message points, a call to action at the end. Easing must be used — nothing moves linearly. Export at 1080×1080 and 9:16.

Thursday UI Design Fundamentals (Figma) ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students can set up components, auto-layout, and a basic design system in Figma
  • Students can design a mobile screen with correct spacing, buttons, and input elements
  • Students understand what makes a mobile UI feel good vs clunky
0:00 – 0:30 · Figma Power Features
Components: like "master styles" — create an element once, use it everywhere. Change the master, all instances update. Auto-layout: like CSS flexbox — elements inside a frame automatically space themselves. Add new content and the container grows. This is essential for designing things that work at different content lengths (e.g. a button with 2-word text vs 5-word text). Design systems: a library of components + colours + text styles that the whole team uses. Show: Figma Community — free design systems from Apple (iOS) and Google (Material) for students to study.
0:30 – 1:00 · Designing a Mobile Screen
Frame size: 390×844 (iPhone 14 size). Spacing rules: 16px minimum margin from screen edges. 8px grid — use multiples of 8 for all spacing (8, 16, 24, 32, 48). Touch targets: minimum 44×44px for any tappable element. Buttons: should clearly look tappable (colour, border, shape). Input fields: should clearly look fillable (border, placeholder text). Hierarchy: what is the ONE thing the user needs to do on this screen? That action should be the most visually prominent element.
1:00 – 1:30 · UI Critique
Show screenshots of several real apps (good and bad examples). For each: Can you find the primary action immediately? Are touch targets obviously interactive? Is there sufficient contrast? Is information hierarchy clear? Students call out specific elements they'd fix and why. This critique language is what UX and UI designers use in team reviews.

Saturday UX Thinking & User Flows ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand what UX actually means in practice — not just making things look pretty
  • Students can map a complete user journey and user flow for a simple app
  • Students can identify friction points and propose design solutions for each
0:00 – 0:30 · What UX Actually Is
UX = User Experience. It's not the same as UI design. UX is concerned with: does this product solve the user's problem? Is it intuitive to use? Where do users get confused or frustrated? UX process: Empathise (understand the user's goals and pain points). Define (what is the core problem to solve?). Ideate (generate solutions). Prototype (quick mockup). Test (user testing — watch someone use it). Then design the UI to serve the UX solution. KEY: you should be able to use an app in the dark, by touch alone — if the layout is truly intuitive, you don't need to read labels.
0:30 – 1:00 · User Journeys & Flows
User journey: the broad experience — from awareness of an app to becoming a loyal user. User flow: the specific step-by-step path through the app to complete one task. Example flow: "User wants to order food" → Open app → Browse restaurants → Select restaurant → Choose items → Review cart → Enter address → Choose payment → Confirm order → Track order. Map this as a flowchart (boxes = screens, diamonds = decisions). Identify: where could a user drop off? Where is there unnecessary friction (too many steps, confusing options)?
1:00 – 1:30 · Map a User Flow
Students map the user flow for their 3-screen app from Thursday. Use Figma or paper — boxes and arrows. Then: identify 3 friction points in their own flow. For each: propose a design solution. Share with the group. Critique: are the friction points real? Are the solutions practical?

  • Give an example of a friction point in an app you use daily. What would you do to fix it?

Monday Design for Print vs Digital ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand CMYK vs RGB, bleed, safe zones, and resolution for print
  • Students can set up a print-ready file in Illustrator or InDesign
  • Students can adapt a digital design for print without it looking wrong
0:00 – 0:30 · Colour Mode & Specs
RGB: screen display. Millions of colours. CMYK: print. 4 ink colours (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Black). The same HEX colour looks different when printed — especially bright neons and vibrant colours, which often shift. ALWAYS check with the printer. Resolution: screen = 72 DPI minimum. Print = 300 DPI minimum at final print size. Bleed: extend background 3mm beyond the edge of the page — for when the printer cuts the paper (cutting lines are never 100% precise). Safe zone: keep important content 5mm inside the trim edge — in case of cutting variation.
0:30 – 1:00 · Setting Up Print Files
In Illustrator: File → New → set colour mode to CMYK, set artboard with bleed (e.g. for A4: 210×297mm + 3mm bleed on all sides). Save as PDF/X-1a or PDF/X-4 for printing (not regular PDF). Outline fonts: text can look wrong at the printer if they don't have your fonts. Type → Create Outlines converts text to vector shapes. Demonstrate a complete A4 flyer setup from scratch.
1:00 – 1:30 · Digital to Print Adaptation
Students take a social media post they designed and adapt it for A4 print. Changes required: resize canvas, add bleed, change colour mode to CMYK, increase font sizes (what reads well on a screen at 1080px often needs to be larger for print), add white space margins. Document every change and why.

Tuesday Pricing, Proposals & Client Communication ▼

Session Objectives

  • Students understand how to price design work (hourly vs project-based vs value-based)
  • Students can write a clear, professional design proposal
  • Students know how to handle scope creep, revisions, and difficult feedback professionally
0:00 – 0:30 · Pricing Your Work
Three pricing models: Hourly (transparent, but clients watch the clock and under-value speed). Project-based (most common — quote a fixed price for a defined scope). Value-based (charge based on the value the work creates for the client, not the hours — used by top-tier designers). Never price below what makes the project sustainable for you. Practical day-rate: hourly rate × 8 = day rate. A logo package = 3–5 days. A brand guide = 5–10 days. A social media template set = 2–3 days.
0:30 – 1:00 · Writing a Proposal
A strong proposal has: Project overview (restate the brief — shows you understood). Proposed solution (what you will deliver). Scope of work (clear deliverables list). What is NOT included (prevents scope creep). Timeline. Investment (price, payment terms, deposit). Acceptance. Never start work without one.
1:00 – 1:30 · Handling Scope Creep & Revisions
Scope creep: client starts adding requests outside the original scope. Response: “I’d love to help with that — that’s outside our original scope, so I’ll send you a separate quote.” Two revision rounds is standard — extra rounds cost extra. When radical changes are requested post-approval: “Changes of this scale would require us to start a new phase — happy to quote that.” Firm but professional.
  • A client asks for “just a few small changes” after the second revision round. How do you respond?
  • What is value-based pricing and when would you use it over hourly rates?

Saturday — CAPSTONE 🏁 Design Showcase & Critique ▼

How to Run the Design Showcase

0:00 – 0:15 · Setup
Display all capstone projects on a shared screen or arranged around the room. Remind students of the feedback format: two specific strengths + one specific, actionable improvement. All feedback must reference a design principle — not just “I like it” or “I don’t like it.”
0:15 – 1:10 · Showcase & Critique
Each student presents their 4-piece portfolio: shows each piece, explains the brief, and walks through one key design decision. Group feedback: two strengths + one improvement. Teacher adds professional context: “In the industry, this would typically be presented as…” or “A client looking at this would ask…” Keep feedback honest and encouraging — growth over perfection.
1:10 – 1:30 · Closing Reflection
Ask each student: what do you now understand about design that you didn’t 4 weeks ago? And: what’s the one skill you want to develop most in the next 3 months? Recognise those who showed the most growth — growth is always more important than raw talent at this stage. Great designers are built, not born.

Teacher Note: Print a simple feedback card for each student with 2–3 bullet points of personalised written feedback. Students remember written praise and specific critique far longer than verbal comments — this small gesture adds enormous value to the programme.

Capstone Portfolio Checklist

  • 4 pieces — each from a different area (logo, brand identity, social media, layout, motion, or UI)
  • Each piece has a written brief (even if fictional — what problem were you solving?)
  • Each piece presented in context using mockups — not just flat design files
  • Colour, typography, and layout principles clearly applied in every piece
  • Files exported at correct resolution for intended use (print or digital)
  • A one-paragraph case study accompanies at least 2 of the 4 pieces
  • Your portfolio feels cohesive — your own design sensibility is visible across all 4 pieces
✏️ Final Self-Assessment

Before the showcase, look at your 4 pieces and honestly answer: (1) Which piece are you most proud of and why? (2) Which piece would you redo if you had more time, and what would you change? (3) What design principle do you now apply automatically that you had to consciously think about in Week 1? These three questions help you articulate your growth — the most impressive thing you can communicate to a future employer or client.
